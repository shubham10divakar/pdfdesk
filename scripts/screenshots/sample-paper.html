Reading Logs as Research Memory

AbstractResearchers read far more papers than they can remember. We describe a simple model of reading as a sequence of sessions, each with a page range, active minutes and a short takeaway, and argue that this log, more than the highlights themselves, is what lets a reader recover why a paper mattered months later. We outline how sessions, page-anchored notes and a revision queue fit together in a local-first workspace.

1 Introduction

A typical literature review involves dozens of PDFs opened over several weeks. File managers record where a paper lives, and annotation tools record what was marked, but neither records the reading itself: when a paper was opened, how far the reader got, and what they concluded at the end of each sitting.

We treat each paper as moving through a lifecycle (inbox, planned, reading, paused, completed, revisit) and each sitting as a session. The session log turns a pile of files into a timeline of attention that can be searched, summarised and revisited.

2 Sessions

A session starts when a paper is opened and ends when the reader leaves it. Only active time counts: the clock pauses when the window is hidden or there has been no input for three minutes. For a session s we store the first and last page reached and the active minutes ms.

coverage(p) = | ⋃s [froms, tos] | / pages(p)

Coverage tells the reader how much of a paper they have actually seen, independent of how long it was left open in a tab.

3 Highlights and notes

Highlights carry a colour with a fixed meaning: key idea, method or result, definition, and open question. Each highlight stores its text and its position relative to the page, so it survives zoom changes and can be re-anchored if the PDF is replaced by a newer version.

Notes are plain Markdown. A reference such as p. 3 becomes a link back to that page, which keeps every claim in the notes one click away from its source.

4 Revision

Past highlights resurface in a daily revision queue. Reading a highlight again, with its page one click away, is cheap, and it is often enough to bring back the argument of the whole paper.

5 Conclusion

A reading log is cheap to collect and valuable to keep. Combined with page-anchored notes and a revision queue, it gives a researcher a memory of their reading that outlasts any single paper.
